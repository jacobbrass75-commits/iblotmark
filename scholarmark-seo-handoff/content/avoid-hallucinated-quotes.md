## Article Body

The scariest AI writing problem is not a weird sentence. You can fix a weird sentence.

The bigger problem is the perfect quote that turns out not to exist.

Maybe the author is real. Maybe the article is real. Maybe the idea sounds close to something you read. But when you search the PDF, check the page, or look up the DOI, the quote falls apart.

That is a hallucinated quote.

## What is a hallucinated quote?

A hallucinated quote is text that an AI tool presents as a real quotation even though the exact wording cannot be found in the source. Sometimes it is attached to a real author. Sometimes it is attached to a real article. Sometimes the source itself is invented.

The risk is not theoretical. Students and instructors regularly describe AI-generated references that look credible but cannot be found. Research has also documented fabricated citations in AI-generated academic-style writing.

The lesson is simple: never let a quote into your final draft unless you can find it in the source.

## Why prompts are not enough

It is tempting to solve the problem by writing a stricter prompt:

"Only use real quotes."

"Do not make anything up."

"Give me exact citations."

Those instructions help, but they are not a verification system. A model can still produce confident text that is wrong. Even source-grounded tools can make mistakes, which is why official guidance from tools like NotebookLM still tells users to double-check output.

For serious academic writing, you need a workflow, not just a better prompt.

## The safer workflow

Use this order:

1. Build the source base.
2. Find the passage.
3. Save the quote with surrounding context.
4. Attach citation notes.
5. Draft from saved evidence.
6. Verify before export.

This flips the usual AI mistake. Instead of asking AI to create a draft and then chasing sources afterward, you start with sources you can inspect.

## Step 1: Build a source base

Collect the PDFs, book chapters, articles, reports, web clips, and notes you are allowed to use. Keep them in one project if possible.

If a source is only an AI suggestion, mark it as unverified. Do not let it become part of your bibliography until you have found it yourself through a library database, publisher page, Google Scholar, Crossref, or another accepted source.

## Step 2: Create a quote bank

For every quote you might use, save:

- exact quoted text
- source title
- author
- page, section, paragraph, timestamp, or location
- surrounding context
- why the quote matters
- the claim it may support
- citation note
- verification status

This makes your draft easier to build and easier to defend.

## Step 3: Draft from the quote bank

Once you have a quote bank, your outline gets easier. Group quotes by theme, turn each theme into a section, and attach evidence to each claim.

This also helps you avoid quote dumping. You are not collecting quotes because they sound impressive. You are collecting evidence because it supports a specific part of your argument.

## Step 4: Verify before export

Before submitting, check every direct quote:

- Does the exact wording appear in the source?
- Is the author, title, year, and page correct?
- Does the surrounding context support your use?
- Is the quote introduced and explained in your own words?
- Is the citation format correct for the assignment?

If any answer is no, pause. Replace the quote, revise the claim, or remove the passage.

## FAQ

### What is a hallucinated quote?

A hallucinated quote is a quote-like sentence that an AI tool presents as real even though the exact wording cannot be found in the original source.

### Can AI invent quotes from real sources?

Yes. AI can generate plausible wording and attach it to a real source or author. Always search the source for the exact wording before using a direct quote.

### How do I check whether an AI quote is real?

Open the original source and search for the exact phrase. If it is a PDF or scan, check the relevant page or section manually as well, because OCR and search can miss text.

### Can source-grounded AI still make mistakes?

Yes. Source grounding reduces risk by making evidence visible, but it does not guarantee perfect output. Always double-check final quotes and citations.

### What should I do if I cannot verify a quote?

Do not use it as a direct quote. Replace it with a verified passage, paraphrase only after checking the source, or remove the claim.
