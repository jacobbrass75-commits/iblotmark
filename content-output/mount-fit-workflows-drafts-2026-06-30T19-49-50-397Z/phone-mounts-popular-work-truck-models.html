Phone Mounts for Popular Work Trucks

It is 6:45 a.m. A driver pulls out of the yard, tosses a phone on the seat, and spends the first ten minutes of the route glancing down at directions instead of watching the road. That is not a driver problem. That is a setup problem. The phone has nowhere to go because the truck was never configured for it.

Work trucks are not passenger cars. An F-250 Super Duty crew cab, a Transit 350 cargo van, and a Sprinter 2500 all have different console shapes, dash materials, cup-holder positions, and upfit rules. A mount that works perfectly in one vehicle may rattle loose, block a vent, or conflict with an upfitter's partition in another. Getting this right matters, especially when FMCSA mobile phone restrictions require commercial drivers to keep devices in a fixed, hands-free position, and NHTSA research consistently ties reaching and glancing to crash risk.

Quick answer: For a single-model fleet, a drill-base AMPS mount gives the most stable, permanent result. For mixed fleets with pickups, vans, and service trucks from multiple brands, cup-holder and suction setups are faster to deploy and easier to move between vehicles. The sections below map each approach to the trucks where it makes the most sense.

How Cab Layout Drives the Mount Decision

Before you order anything, look at three things in each vehicle: where the driver's eyes naturally rest, whether the console has a usable cup holder, and whether the dash surface is flat enough for suction or textured enough to need a disc. Those three factors narrow the field faster than any spec sheet.

Ford F-Series trucks, particularly the F-250 and F-350 built on the Ford Pro chassis cab platform, have a wide center console with deep cup holders and a relatively flat lower dash. Both cup-holder and drill-base setups work well here. The Chevrolet Silverado HD and GMC Sierra HD share a similar console layout, though the cup-holder diameter varies by trim, so measuring before ordering matters. Ram pickups and Ram chassis cab variants often have a taller, narrower console stack that positions a cup-holder mount slightly higher than in a Ford, which some drivers prefer for sightline reasons.

Vans are a different story. Transit and Sprinter cabs are narrower and more upright. The dash is closer to the driver, which makes suction mounts on the windshield or dash face practical. ProMaster cabs have a car-like dash with a lower instrument cluster, and the cup holders sit far forward on the console, making a tall cup-holder mount awkward for some drivers. For medium-duty vocational trucks like the Freightliner M2 106 Plus or cutaway bodies built on the Chevrolet Express Cutaway platform, the cab is often stripped of consumer-style cup holders entirely, and a drill-base AMPS mount on the dash or a flat panel is the only reliable option.

Fleet vs. Owner-Operator: Which Approach Fits

Fleet managers running a uniform model year across all vehicles get the most value from a standardized drill-base install. One AMPS pattern, one installation procedure, one training step. Owner-operators running a single truck for years often prefer the same approach because permanence means the mount is never missing when the shift starts.

Mixed fleets are a different calculation. If your yard has F-250s, Transits, and a couple of Sprinters, a cup-holder or suction setup that moves between vehicles without tools is more practical than drilling every cab differently. Vehicle-specific mounts from OEM accessory programs can be a strong fit when every truck is the same model year, but they become a sourcing headache the moment the fleet adds a different brand or a newer model year with a redesigned console.

The honest answer is that no single mount type is best for every truck. The matrix above is a starting point. Measure the cup holders, look at the dash surface, and check whether upfit rules or partition installs restrict where hardware can go before committing to a solution.

FAQ

Will a cup-holder mount work in a Ford Transit cargo van?
It can, but Transit cup holders sit far forward on the console, which puts the phone outside a comfortable sightline for most drivers. A suction mount on the dash face is usually a better fit in Transit cabs.
Do I need to drill to use an AMPS mount?
Yes, AMPS drill-base mounts require four bolts into a flat surface. If drilling is not an option, the suction cup or cup-holder versions use the same AMPS ball joint system without any permanent installation.
Can one mount move between a pickup and a van in the same fleet?
A cup-holder mount can move between vehicles if both have compatible cup-holder diameters. A suction mount is even more portable since it attaches to any smooth surface without tools.
What does AMPS compatibility mean for a fleet?
AMPS is an industry-standard four-hole bolt pattern used across hundreds of mount accessories. Choosing AMPS-based mounts means arms, cradles, and bases from different products can be mixed and matched as needs change.
